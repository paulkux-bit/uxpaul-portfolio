<div className="eyebrow">The problem</div>

## An analyst has to be certain before they act. The AI gives them a hunch.

**An alert fires: the AI thinks it has found an enemy command node.** The analyst has minutes, not hours.

**Two ways to be wrong, both expensive.** Act on a false flag and you spend assets, or cue a strike on the wrong thing. Sit on a real one and the picture moves on without you.

**The machine is confident. The analyst is accountable.** A model can be wrong at no cost. The person who acts on it cannot.

**And certainty has to come from this.** Every intelligence discipline reporting at once, thousands of entities across a whole theater, arriving faster than anyone can read. The signal that matters is in there, somewhere, and the clock is running.

<div className="eyebrow">What I found</div>

## How much uncertainty a user will tolerate changes with the tempo of the war.

I ran discovery in two places at once: inside the doctrine, and in the field beside the analysts who live by it. Good intel analysts don't trust data blindly. They weigh it, argue with it, and own the call, and getting to design for that judgment was the privilege of the project. Interviews ran from junior operators up to flag officers.

The finding that reorganized the project was not about a feature. It was about trust. Analysts do not hold one standard for believing a piece of intelligence. They hold three, and the tempo of the fight decides which one is in force.

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">The bet</div>

## I designed the AI to open a case, not close one.

The obvious move with a capable model is to let it answer. Surface the conclusion, badge it, and let the analyst accept or dismiss. That is the pattern most tools reach for, and it is the pattern that produces black-box mistrust and, under pressure, analysis paralysis.

I bet the other way. In FDT-E, the AI is not permitted to hand you an answer. When it detects something, it opens a case, and a case has to be worked. The design turns every anomaly into a short, guided investigation whose steps are lifted from the doctrine analysts already answer to.

That last part was the non-obvious call, and it came from a conversation I keep returning to. A former adversary-army officer, now a subject-matter expert supporting U.S. forces, told the team:

> "In the Russian army, we didn't just read your doctrine; we mastered it, often better than your own forces seemed to. The problem is that your own forces don't always read or follow it."
>
> – Former adversary-army officer, U.S. Army Indo-Pacom subject-matter expert

Doctrine was already the shared language of correctness. The adversary had studied it; our own tempo sometimes buried it. So instead of writing a verification flow from scratch and asking analysts to trust my logic, I shaped the flow out of theirs. The steps the tool walks you through are the doctrinal steps for confirming a threat. Following the software and following the doctrine became the same act. I call the pattern the **verification gate**: the fixed, doctrine-shaped checkpoint an AI claim has to pass through before a human carries it forward.

</section>

<h3 className="text-h3 mt-12 mb-4">A "why" before a "what."</h3>

Open the anomaly and the first thing the design gives you is provenance, not a conclusion. It names what the model suspects, a potential C2 node, and shows the disciplines the suspicion is built from before it asks anything of you. Seeing that the read came from open-source and communications intelligence is what dissolves the black box. The model is no longer asserting; it is showing its work.

<h3 className="text-h3 mt-12 mb-4">The verification steps are the doctrine.</h3>

Choosing to verify drops the analyst into a guided, all-source correlation. The tool pulls relevant hits from each intelligence discipline over the window that matters, scores them, and lets the analyst rescan as the picture moves. The step names, all-source correlation, fusion, collaborative verification, are not interface inventions. They are the doctrinal sequence for confirming a threat, with a link to the reference sitting right in the step. Procedural compliance stops being a separate chore and becomes the path of least resistance.

<h3 className="text-h3 mt-12 mb-4">The human escalates. The software only makes the case.</h3>

When the case holds, the tool moves to response, and here it stays deliberately subordinate. It drafts the escalation, elevate the alert, push to the common operational picture, cue countermeasures, and hands the analyst the pen. Coordination happens in line, with the other watch standers who will act on the call. The design proposes; the person decides and disseminates. That division is the point of the whole system.

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">What I got wrong</div>

## The richest picture was the wrong one.

The first serious build was the impressive one. Live sensor streaming, everything fused, motion on the map, the whole disposition breathing in real time. It demoed beautifully. Then we took it to a Red Flag exercise and ran it against real streaming data in the conditions it would meet at the edge, and it fell apart. Not because the analysis was wrong. Because the environment could not carry it. Denied, degraded, intermittent, and limited bandwidth is the norm at the edge, and the richest version of the picture was the first thing to die on a starved connection.

I had believed, without examining it, that more fidelity was more help. The field corrected me in an afternoon. The version that survived is the quieter one, the one that leads with a single confident claim and lets the analyst pull on more only when the link and the moment allow. Restraint turned out to be the operational requirement, not the aesthetic preference.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">What I can't show you</div>

## The real FDT-E lives on the high side, and it is a better app than the one on this page.

We designed and built in an unclassified environment and deployed into a secured one. I was not ready for the gap. The volume, the velocity, and the variance of the data on the classified network are past anything the unclassified build ever had to hold, and the tool grew richer and sharper to meet it. What you are reading is the shadow of the system, drawn with notional data. The version operators use is the better one, and it is the one I cannot show.

I have made peace with that being the honest shape of defense work. The evidence stays behind the fence. What travels is the thinking.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">Where it is now</div>

## It's in active use across multiple major commands.

That reach is the outcome I hold onto, because it was not a given. FDT-E supports dozens of mission types, including ones as consequential as strike and search-and-rescue, and it keeps taking on more. It has become a platform other systems want to plug into, with integration requests arriving from elsewhere in the department. And on the thing the project was built for, operators report more confidence working through ambiguity, because the workflow and the transparency give them somewhere to stand when the data will not settle.

I finished my work in 2025.
