<div className="eyebrow">The problem</div>

## An analyst has to be certain before they act. The AI gives them a hunch.

**An alert fires: the AI thinks it has found an enemy command node.** The analyst has minutes, not hours.

**Two ways to be wrong, both expensive.** Act on a false flag and you spend assets, or cue a strike on the wrong thing. Sit on a real one and the picture moves on without you.

**The machine is confident. The analyst is accountable.** A model can be wrong at no cost. The person who acts on it cannot.

**And that certainty has to come out of a flood:** every discipline reporting at once, faster than anyone can read.

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">The bet</div>

## How do you trust what you can't question?

**Don't make the machine convincing. Make it checkable.** Everyone wanted a smarter model. I thought the question was whether an analyst would trust it. They won't act on an answer they can't check.

In workshops and interviews the same thing kept coming up: they already trust doctrine. The machine didn't need to win them over. It needed to speak doctrine. So that's what we built.

**Doctrine binds the machine, not the analyst.** Past the checklist, they can escalate, get a second opinion, or trust their own read. The person makes the call.

FDT-E never hands down a verdict. Every call clears a doctrine gate first.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">What I got wrong</div>

## The richest picture was the wrong one.

The first serious build was the impressive one. Live sensor streaming, everything fused, motion on the map, the whole disposition breathing in real time. It demoed beautifully. Then we took it to a Red Flag exercise and ran it against real streaming data in the conditions it would meet at the edge, and it fell apart. Not because the analysis was wrong. Because the environment could not carry it. Denied, degraded, intermittent, and limited bandwidth is the norm at the edge, and the richest version of the picture was the first thing to die on a starved connection.

I had believed, without examining it, that more fidelity was more help. The field corrected me in an afternoon. The version that survived is the quieter one, the one that leads with a single confident claim and lets the analyst pull on more only when the link and the moment allow. Restraint turned out to be the operational requirement, not the aesthetic preference.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">What I can't show you</div>

## The real FDT-E lives on the high side, and it is a better app than the one on this page.

We designed and built in an unclassified environment and deployed into a secured one. I was not ready for the gap. The volume, the velocity, and the variance of the data on the classified network are past anything the unclassified build ever had to hold, and the tool grew richer and sharper to meet it. What you are reading is the shadow of the system, drawn with notional data. The version operators use is the better one, and it is the one I cannot show.

I have made peace with that being the honest shape of defense work. The evidence stays behind the fence. What travels is the thinking.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">Where it is now</div>

## It outgrew what we built it for.

That reach is the outcome I hold onto, because it was not a given. FDT-E supports dozens of mission types, including ones as consequential as strike and search-and-rescue, and it keeps taking on more. It has become a platform other systems want to plug into, with integration requests arriving from elsewhere in the department. And on the thing the project was built for, operators report more confidence working through ambiguity, because the workflow and the transparency give them somewhere to stand when the data will not settle.

I finished my work in 2025.
