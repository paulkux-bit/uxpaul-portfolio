<div className="eyebrow">The problem</div>

## An analyst has to be certain before they act. The AI gives them a hunch.

**An alert fires: the AI thinks it has found an enemy command node.** The analyst has minutes, not hours.

**Two ways to be wrong, both expensive.** Act on a false flag and you spend assets, or cue a strike on the wrong thing. Sit on a real one and the picture moves on without you.

**The machine is confident. The analyst is accountable.** A model can be wrong at no cost. The person who acts on it cannot.

**And that certainty has to come out of a flood:** every discipline reporting at once, faster than anyone can read.

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">The bet</div>

## How do you trust what you can't question?

**Don't make the machine convincing. Make it checkable.** Everyone wanted a smarter model. I thought the question was whether an analyst would trust it. They won't act on an answer they can't check.

In workshops and interviews the same thing kept coming up: they already trust doctrine. The machine didn't need to win them over. It needed to speak doctrine. So that's what we built.

**Doctrine binds the machine, not the analyst.** Past the checklist, they can escalate, get a second opinion, or trust their own read. The person makes the call.

FDT-E never hands down a verdict. Every call clears a doctrine gate first.

</section>

<div className="eyebrow">What it took</div>

## The model got the credit. The schema did the work.

<p className="section-lede">An agent can only flag what it can find, name, timestamp. Before anomaly detection was worth anything, three unglamorous things had to be right underneath it: **search, metadata, freshness.**</p>

**Search came first, because nothing downstream works without it.** I specified it story by story: multi-select, select-all, autocomplete, grouping, drawing an area on the map. Every state drawn before anyone wrote a query.

<BentoBand
  src="/case-studies/us-navy-fdt-e/foundations/search-ia--band.jpg"

  caption="Search, specified story by story."
  gloss="Multi-select, autocomplete, grouping, area draw."
  aspect={2.426}
/>

An analyst who cannot narrow the picture cannot ask the model a useful question either. Search was the vocabulary for both.

**Then the metadata, one equipment type at a time.** A radar and a patrol vessel do not describe themselves with the same fields. Getting that right meant workshops with operators and subject-matter experts, arguing field by field over what actually identifies a thing and what is just noise on the record. It was the slowest work on the project.

<BentoBand
  src="/case-studies/us-navy-fdt-e/reflection/reflection-provenance--band.png"
  alt="A record popover opened over the FDT-E watchlist table: emitter parameters for a tracked patrol vessel beside a timeline map of where the track has been."
  caption="Metadata tailored to the equipment."
  gloss="Emitter parameters, and where the track has been."
  aspect={2.402}
/>

One confident claim on the surface, the provenance one click under it. Tailored fields are what let the model say this is unusual for this kind of thing, rather than unusual in general.

**Then freshness, which is where I was wrong.** The first serious build was the impressive one. Live sensor streaming, everything fused, motion on the map, the whole disposition breathing in real time. **It demoed beautifully, and it died at Red Flag.**

**The analysis was never the problem. The environment was.** Denied, degraded, intermittent, limited bandwidth is the norm at the edge, and the richest version of the picture was the first thing to go on a starved connection. I had believed, without examining it, that more fidelity was more help. The field corrected me in an afternoon.

So the disposition stopped moving and started reporting. What changed, when, and on whose authority, in text a starved link can carry.

<BentoBand
  src="/case-studies/us-navy-fdt-e/reflection/reflection-activity--band.png"

Everything else became something the analyst pulls, not something the tool pushes. Restraint was the operational requirement, not the aesthetic preference.

**None of this is the part people ask about.** The anomaly agent got the attention. It worked because it was searching a schema that knew what everything was and when it last changed.

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">What I can't show you</div>

## The real FDT-E lives on the high side, and it is a better app than the one on this page.

We designed and built in an unclassified environment and deployed into a secured one. I was not ready for the gap. The volume, the velocity, and the variance of the data on the classified network are past anything the unclassified build ever had to hold, and the tool grew richer and sharper to meet it. What you are reading is the shadow of the system, drawn with notional data. The version operators use is the better one, and it is the one I cannot show.

I have made peace with that. The evidence stays behind the fence. What travels is the thinking.

**What I can tell you is how far it went.** Operators report more confidence working through ambiguity, because the workflow and the transparency give them somewhere to stand when the data will not settle.

I handed the work off in 2025.
