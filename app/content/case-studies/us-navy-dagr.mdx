## The go or no go turns on one assessment.

**Before a mission flies, the unit gathers in one room.** Pilots, maintenance crew, operations planners, flight logistics, and Wilbur, the intel analyst.

**Wilbur's turn is the threat picture.** Where the enemy is, what can see the aircraft, and from what altitude. He briefs what he knows, and what he does not.

**If the picture is wrong, an aircraft is lost or gives away its position, and the mission fails.** The commander weighs it against everything else in the room and makes the call.

## How do you keep an answer from going stale?

<p className="section-lede">Bring the live data to the math instead of typing it in.</p>

The calculators already existed, inside a classified analysis engine our product team was not cleared to see into. We did not need to see into it. What it lacked was a feed: run it by hand, and you supply every input yourself.

Dagr already held the tasking orders, the maps and the tracked disposition. So Line of Sight kept the engine's math and changed what reached it: live positions, the mission's own route, and the altitudes the analyst wants checked.

I also structured it for the analyses that did not exist yet. An analysis became an object, and every one of them deposits layers into a list the app already had.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## One radar, one altitude, one shape.

<p className="section-lede">The analyst taps a threat on the map and asks it a question.</p>

The panel wants two things: how high the radar's antenna sits, and which altitudes to check. Submit, and the answer comes back as a polygon. That is the ground the radar can see from that height, cut by every ridge in between.

Analysts rarely want one altitude. A maximum, a minimum and a step generate the whole band in a single submit.

Each answer is a layer. The analyst can hide it, recolor it to match their unit's legend, or mark it primary. Primary is the recommendation.

## The picture goes out of date before takeoff.

<p className="section-lede">Missions arrive up to seventy-two hours before they fly.</p>

**So the answer had to come looking for the analyst.** The equipment in that picture moves during those three days, so Dagr reads the tasking order and runs the analysis again close to takeoff. What is waiting for the analyst is the current answer, not the one he asked for on Monday.

The engine runs somewhere else, and for a forward-deployed unit the link to it degrades. So the interface has to be plain about which state it is in: working, answered, or failed.

## An analyst looked at the shape and said he couldn't brief it.

<p className="section-lede">Testing with real data, in the classified lab.</p>

**The polygons were right in structure and wrong in geometry.** I specified them flat, in Figma. They render on a globe, and a line of sight bends with the earth. The shapes came back the wrong size in the wrong places, and in this work the geometry is the entire answer.

<blockquote className="pull-quote pull-quote--quiet">
  <p>“This is something I can't brief.”</p>
  <cite>Intel analyst, test session</cite>
</blockquote>

He was not making a point about precision. He meant that standing in front of the commander with it would cost him something he needed. Engineers and their subject matter experts did the math, and we art directed the render together over a surge of a few sprints, because the correction was global.

It also changed things nobody had built yet. The drawing tools were re-specified so that anything an analyst draws has the earth's curvature applied automatically.

## Where Dagr stands.

<div className="milestone">
  <p className="milestone__frame">Fielded through Red Flag and in use at unit level</p>
  <p className="milestone__date">2023</p>
  <p className="milestone__sub">Defunded in budget cuts after I left.</p>

Getting fielded is not a deploy date. It means commanders, weapons officers and planners took it into their own stack during a live exercise and were willing to work behind it.

Then more aircraft communities wanted their own modeling inside it than nine people could build, and the money went somewhere else.

**I went in believing the calculation was the unlock.** It was most of it. What surprised me was how much of the work downstream was judgment. Mission planning is an art, and accurate data mostly buys people the room to practice it.
