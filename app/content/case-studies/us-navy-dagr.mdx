## One assessment can turn a go into a no-go.

Before a mission flies, the unit gathers in one room: pilots, maintenance, operations, logistics, and Wilbur, the intel analyst.

**The threat picture is Wilbur's.** He tells them where the enemy is, what can see the aircraft, and from what altitude. He briefs what he knows and what he does not.

**If the picture is wrong, an aircraft gives away its position or does not come back.** The commander weighs it against everything else and makes the call.

<section className="cs-section">

## How do you give an analyst back the hours he spends typing?

<p className="section-lede">Start the analysis where the data already is.</p>

The smart people at Idaho National Laboratory built the line of sight model, and the math is classified. Our team was never going to see inside it, and did not need to. What we owed Wilbur was everything around it. **Anything the app already knows is something he should not type.**

I had already built the live feeds on DoD-approved map layers. So the map is where a Line of Sight analysis starts.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## He can afford to ask twice.

<p className="section-lede">The question is always the same. From what altitude can that radar see us?</p>

Wilbur asks it on the map. He taps the radar, supplies an altitude, and gets back the ground that radar covers from that height, terrain already accounted for. Nothing to look up first.

One altitude is rarely the answer, so he asks for the range the mission might fly and gets every step inside it back at once, each its own layer, in his unit's colors.

Holding two of them against each other is a click, not a rebuild. **That comparing is the analysis, and it is what the old model never left him time to do.**

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## The work was deciding what he never has to see.

<p className="section-lede">Line of Sight is fast because two other things came first.</p>

The analysis needs to know where the threats are and what the equipment at them can do. I built one search that covers both. Every threat comes back with its own numbers attached, which is why the panel already knew the antenna height was 59.64 feet.

<IngestBento />

The search does not run once. The area stays under watch, and Dagr reads the tasking order to run the analysis again near takeoff, so what he opens on the day is not the picture he built on Monday.

A national search returns far more than one mission cares about. So the second thing I built was the filter. He narrows to his airframe, then down to the equipment types, until only the threats this mission has to account for are left. That is what he briefs.

<FiltersBento />

The search and the filter were never only for Line of Sight. Together they mean the picture he opens is already his: his mission, his airframe, the handful of threats that matter. **I was not just building a feature. I was building what the next one would sit on.**

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## An analyst looked at the shape and said he couldn't brief it.

<p className="section-lede">Testing with real data, in the classified lab.</p>

**The polygons were right in structure and wrong in geometry.** I specified them flat, in Figma. They render on a globe, and a line of sight bends with the earth. The shapes came back the wrong size in the wrong places, and in this work the geometry is the entire answer.

<blockquote className="pull-quote pull-quote--quiet">
  <p>“This is something I can't brief.”</p>
  <cite>Intel analyst, test session</cite>
</blockquote>

Standing in front of the commander with that shape would cost him something he needed. Engineers and their subject matter experts did the math, and we art directed the render together over a surge of a few sprints, because the correction was global.

The drawing tools changed too. Anything an analyst draws now has the earth's curvature applied automatically.

## Where Dagr stands.

<div className="milestone">
  <p className="milestone__frame">Fielded through Red Flag, the Air Force's large-force exercise, and in use at unit level</p>
  <p className="milestone__date">2023</p>
  <p className="milestone__sub">Defunded in budget cuts after I left.</p>
</div>

Getting fielded is not a deploy date. It means commanders, weapons officers and planners took it into their own stack during a live exercise and were willing to work behind it.

Then more aircraft communities wanted their own modeling inside it than nine people could build, and the money went somewhere else.

**I went in believing the calculation was the unlock.** It was most of it. What surprised me was how much of the work downstream was judgment. Mission planning is an art, and accurate data mostly buys people the room to practice it.

</section>
