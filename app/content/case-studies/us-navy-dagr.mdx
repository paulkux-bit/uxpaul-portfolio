## One assessment can turn a go into a no-go.

Before a mission flies, the unit gathers in one room: pilots, maintenance, operations, logistics, and Wilbur, the intel analyst.

The threat picture is Wilbur's. He tells them where the enemy is, what can see the aircraft, and from what altitude. He briefs what he knows and what he does not.

**If the picture is wrong, an aircraft gives away its position or does not come back.** The commander weighs it against everything else and makes the call.

## How do you give an analyst back the hours he spends typing?

<p className="section-lede">Start the analysis where the data already is.</p>

The smart people at Idaho National Laboratory built the line of sight model, and the math is classified. Our team was never going to see inside it, and did not need to. What we owed Wilbur was everything around it. **Anything the app already knows is something he should not type.**

I had already built the live feeds on DoD-approved map layers. So the map is where a Line of Sight analysis starts.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## He can afford to ask twice.

<p className="section-lede">The question is always the same. From what altitude can that radar see us?</p>

Wilbur asks it on the map. He taps the radar, supplies an altitude, and gets back the ground that radar covers from that height, terrain already accounted for. Nothing to look up first.

One altitude is rarely the answer, so he asks for the range the mission might fly and gets every step inside it back at once, each its own layer, in his unit's colors.

Holding two of them against each other is a click, not a rebuild. **That comparing is the analysis, and it is what the old model never left him time to do.**

<section className="cs-section">

## The work was deciding what he never has to see.

<p className="section-lede">Line of Sight is fast because two other things came first.</p>

The analysis needs two things: where the threats sit and what their equipment can do. I built one search that covers both. That is why the panel already knew the antenna height was 59.64 feet.

<IngestBento />

The search does not run once. It reruns near takeoff, so the picture is never Monday's.

A national search returns far more than one mission cares about. So the second thing I built was the filter. He narrows to his airframe, then to the equipment types, until only what this mission must account for is left. That is what he briefs.

<FiltersBento />

The search and the filter were never only for Line of Sight. The picture he opens is already his: his mission, his airframe, the threats that matter. **I was not just building a feature. I was building what the next one would sit on.**

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## An analyst looked at the shape and said he couldn't brief it.

<p className="section-lede">Where the design met the real system.</p>

**The polygons were right in structure and wrong in geometry.** I designed them unclassified, in Figma. They ran classified, on real geospatial data and real physics, rendered on a globe. A line of sight bends with the earth, so the shapes came back the wrong size in the wrong places.

The engineers had the math. I art directed the render until the shapes were right. The fix sat below the feature, so anything an analyst draws now bends with the earth.
