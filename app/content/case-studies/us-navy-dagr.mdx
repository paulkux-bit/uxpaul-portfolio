## One assessment can turn a go into a no-go.

Before a mission flies, the unit gathers in one room, and the plan waits on Wilbur, the intel analyst.

He owns the threat picture, which answers one question: at what altitude can the enemy see us? He briefs what he knows and what he does not.

**If the answer is wrong, the aircraft gives away its position or does not come back.**

## One hand-built picture failed Wilbur in three ways.

<p className="section-lede">I interviewed over seventy intel analysts across Air Combat Command, at units flying different airframes, and watched many of them build a threat picture from scratch.</p>

<FrictionMoment work="homer-fog-warning" />

Wilbur found the threat data in separate systems and typed every value into the legacy tool by hand. None of that time was analysis. The tool knew only what he typed, and enemy equipment stayed where he last put it, even after it moved. What he could not confirm in time went on the brief as risk. Aircrew trusted him with their lives, and each unknown made that trust harder to keep.

</section>

<section className="cs-section cs-section--turn">

## How do you give an analyst back the hours he spends typing?

<p className="section-lede turn-answer">Start the analysis <span className="turn-answer__key">where the data already is.</span></p>

He should not have to type anything the app already knows. I designed the live feeds on DoD-approved map layers, so Line of Sight starts on the map.

Idaho National Laboratory built the model behind it, and the math is classified. Our team was never going to see inside it, and did not need to. What we owed Wilbur was everything around it.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## I designed Line of Sight so Wilbur can ask as many times as he needs.

<p className="section-lede">The question is always the same: at what altitude can that radar see us?</p>

One altitude is rarely enough. He needs the full range the mission might fly, and the old tool never gave him time to ask twice.

So the app fills in the radar's details for him. One entry returns every altitude in the range, with the terrain accounted for. Every answer stays on the map, because **comparing them is the analysis.**

<AnalysisBento />

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## I designed the search and the filter that Line of Sight builds on.

<p className="section-lede">Before Line of Sight could answer anything, it had to know where the threats were and which ones mattered.</p>

The search finds where the threats sit and what their equipment can do. It runs again near takeoff, so the map shows equipment where it is now, not where he last put it.

<IngestBento />

The filter keeps only the threats that can hurt his aircraft, because a helicopter and an F-22 face different ones. What is left is what he briefs.

<FiltersBento />

The search and the filter were never only for Line of Sight. Route view, the next feature, was designed on them too.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## Real data broke my mockups, so I art‑directed the shapes until they were right.

<p className="section-lede">An analyst looked at the first shapes and said he could not brief them.</p>

The shapes were right in what they showed and wrong in where they sat. I had designed them unclassified, in Figma, on a flat map. On real data, rendered on a globe, they came back the wrong size in the wrong places, because a radar cannot see past the curve of the Earth. Briefed as they were, those shapes would have cost him the aircrew's trust.

The engineers had the math, and they implemented the fix before anything was fielded. It went into the map itself, so anything an analyst draws now follows the Earth's curve.
