## One assessment can turn a go into a no-go.

Before a mission flies, the unit gathers in one room: pilots, maintenance, operations, logistics, and Wilbur, the intel analyst.

**The threat picture is Wilbur's.** He tells them where the enemy is, what can see the aircraft, and from what altitude. He briefs what he knows, and what he does not.

**If the picture is wrong, an aircraft gives away its position or does not come back.** The commander weighs it against everything else and makes the call.

## How do you give an analyst back the hours he spends typing?

<p className="section-lede">Start the analysis where the data already is.</p>

The smart people at Idaho National Laboratory built the line of sight model, and the math is classified. Our team was never going to see inside it, and did not need to. What we owed Wilbur was everything around it that made it easy to use. **Anything the app already knows is something he should not type.**

I had already built the live feeds onto DoD approved map layers. So the map is where a Line of Sight analysis starts.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## From a tap on the map to a shape he can brief.

<p className="section-lede">The radar he taps brings its own numbers. He supplies the altitude.</p>

Submit, and the answer comes back as a polygon, the ground that radar can see from that height, cut by every ridge in between.

Analysts rarely want one altitude. A maximum, a minimum and a threshold generate the whole band in a single submit.

Each answer is a layer. He can hide it, recolor it to his unit's legend, or mark one as the mission altitude. Comparing two of them is a click rather than a rebuild, and that is the part of the job that is actually analysis.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## He stopped going out to get the threat data.

<p className="section-lede">One search across the national records, filtered to his airframe.</p>

Line of Sight needed two things: where the threats are, and what the equipment at them can do. The live feed brought both, and every threat arrived carrying its own characteristics. That is why the panel already knew the antenna height was 59.64 feet. Wilbur never looked it up.

<IngestBento />

**Most of what arrives does not matter to the mission he is briefing.** He filters it down to what matters to this aircraft. What is left is small enough to work through, and working through it is the brief.

<FiltersBento />

The next step was to start from the route instead of the radar. A fighter and a helicopter fly into different risks, so the mission and the airframe should choose the threats, and choose them fast.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

## The picture goes out of date before takeoff.

<p className="section-lede">Missions arrive up to seventy-two hours before they fly.</p>

**So the answer had to come looking for the analyst.** The equipment in that picture moves during those three days, so Dagr reads the tasking order and runs the analysis again close to takeoff. What is waiting for the analyst is the current answer, not the one he asked for on Monday.

The engine runs somewhere else, and for a forward-deployed unit the link to it degrades. So the interface has to be plain about which state it is in: working, answered, or failed.

## An analyst looked at the shape and said he couldn't brief it.

<p className="section-lede">Testing with real data, in the classified lab.</p>

**The polygons were right in structure and wrong in geometry.** I specified them flat, in Figma. They render on a globe, and a line of sight bends with the earth. The shapes came back the wrong size in the wrong places, and in this work the geometry is the entire answer.

<blockquote className="pull-quote pull-quote--quiet">
  <p>“This is something I can't brief.”</p>
  <cite>Intel analyst, test session</cite>
</blockquote>

Standing in front of the commander with that shape would cost him something he needed. Engineers and their subject matter experts did the math, and we art directed the render together over a surge of a few sprints, because the correction was global.

The drawing tools changed too. Anything an analyst draws now has the earth's curvature applied automatically.

## Where Dagr stands.

<div className="milestone">
  <p className="milestone__frame">Fielded through Red Flag, the Air Force's large-force exercise, and in use at unit level</p>
  <p className="milestone__date">2023</p>
  <p className="milestone__sub">Defunded in budget cuts after I left.</p>
</div>

Getting fielded is not a deploy date. It means commanders, weapons officers and planners took it into their own stack during a live exercise and were willing to work behind it.

Then more aircraft communities wanted their own modeling inside it than nine people could build, and the money went somewhere else.

**I went in believing the calculation was the unlock.** It was most of it. What surprised me was how much of the work downstream was judgment. Mission planning is an art, and accurate data mostly buys people the room to practice it.
