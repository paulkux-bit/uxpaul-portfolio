## Somebody is already waiting for the dress you just sent back.

<p className="section-lede">Nuuly rents clothes by subscription: six garments a month, then six more.</p>

**Her next box ships on a date she picked.** The dress is still in a return tote.

**Each turn costs two shipping legs, a wash, an inspection, and a repair.** All of it out of one monthly fee. Wash it wrong and the dress is gone. Move it slowly and her box ships short.

## The menu is for the station, not the person

<p className="section-lede">A Return Specialist works Returning. A Merchandise Specialist works Receiving. Nobody browses.</p>

**So the seven apps are not a menu a person shops.** They are a list of jobs a station can be given.

**The flexibility lives in the furniture. The software follows it.** Roll a cart to another part of the floor, give it different work, pick the app that matches. That let every app stay narrow and specific to one task instead of becoming one general tool that asks its user which mode they are in.

Receiving, Fingerprint, Test Wash, Measure, Inspection, Returning, Packing.

## The hard part wasn't the screen

**It was knowing which bin.** A returned garment has to go into the right cleaning process, and the interface for that is one screen: scan the item, and it tells you which colored bin at your station to drop it in. There is nothing to learn and nothing to decide.

<BentoBand
  src="/case-studies/nuuly/bins-first.png"
  alt="Sort to wash bins, scanned one of six: the first garment has a bin code, the other five are still unread."
  caption="One garment in."
  gloss="Five still unread. The tote is worked one scan at a time."

Getting that answer right took months. I worked on a wash-cycle discovery project that ran thousands of actual machine washes, measuring garments before and after, learning how different materials behaved in the industrial machines commissioned for the building. The data science team built a model from the results. The model is what answers when the scanner fires.

Two of the seven apps are that project, made permanent. Test Wash runs a new style through a cycle and records what happened. Measure compares a garment before and after washing so the team can tell how many wears it has left before it stops being worth renting. The research did not end in a document. It ended in software that keeps doing the research.

## I designed for the hundredth garment

The instinct in a tool nobody has used before is to make it teachable. I went the other way and designed for recall instead of discovery, because the warehouse manager had built a training environment that already taught the shift: how the stations interlock, what the process is, where a garment goes next. The app did not have to explain the operation. It only had to handle the task in front of it. That was somebody else's investment paying for my decision, and it is the reason the decision was available at all.

So the primary action never moves. Same corner, same place, every app, every screen, so the hand learns it and stops looking.

**The hit area runs past the paint, out to the bezel.** The button you see respects the same margin as everything else on the screen. The button you can hit does not stop there. A screen edge is a physical bound, so a hand thrown at it cannot overshoot, and the target becomes effectively unmissable in the direction of travel. It can be acquired without looking, which matters because the specialist is looking at a dress. Whether they hold the scanner left or right stops mattering too.

The cost is that the best part of it is invisible. Somebody who looks at the screen sees an ordinary button in an ordinary place. And the whole thing depends on a rule holding: nothing else may live in that margin, on any screen, in any of the seven apps, or the most important action in the app starts collecting mis-taps.

## I built bright mode. They stayed on dim.

Warehouse lighting is not one thing. It changes by bay, by hour, by season. So I built two modes and let the specialist choose, and I worked with the Android developers to understand what the monitors could do at each end. I was proud of bright mode.

Hardly anyone used it. They stayed on dim.

I still do not know why. It might be what they were trained on. It might be that bright was not bright enough to be worth the switch. It might be that dim was simply the nicer thing to look at in that room, and that was enough. What I am fairly sure of is that I solved for a variable I had assumed was the problem, and the people doing the work had already settled it another way.
