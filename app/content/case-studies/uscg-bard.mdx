# Every recreational boating fatality in the United States is counted in one system.

After twenty years, the system that counts them had stopped serving the people who feed it.

BARD, the Boating Accident Report Database, is the federal system of record for every reportable recreational boating incident in the country. Fifty-six jurisdictions file into it. The Coast Guard publishes the national statistics it produces, and those numbers set safety policy, award grants, and defend federal rulemaking. I led the design of its first ground-up replacement in two decades.

## The person at the other end of the form

Maria Chen is a proto-persona: a composite drawn from the twenty jurisdiction points-of-contact I interviewed, reality-checked against one real source. That source is a former Florida boating-safety coordinator who personally managed every accident report for the state for twelve years before moving to Coast Guard headquarters. Maria is one of more than twenty data staff on the Florida side of BARD, the highest-volume jurisdiction in the country. Her day is not one form. It is a queue: pull the morning's batch, run the import, work the cases that came back flagged, fix what the Coast Guard returned overnight, file the "no incidents" affirmation for the rural counties that did not report, then start the next batch. She moves fifteen to thirty cases in a single import session, several sessions a week. She is not analyzing. She is moving cases.

The worst moment in her week is a rejection. When the Coast Guard returns a case, the email names the case but rarely the field. A report can be marked "complete" with a required answer left blank, such as whether a fatality victim was wearing a life jacket, and the rejection reason gets typed into a comment box on a screen Maria never sees. So she has a rejected case, a vague reason, no field-level pointer, and a sixty-day federal clock that started the day the incident was reported. She hunts. She resubmits. She waits to see whether it bounces again.

<PopUp anchor="stakes">
The stake that stuck with me: Florida law enforcement uses BARD summary sheets as evidence in depositions. When what an officer entered does not match what the sheet printed, the officer ends up under oath explaining a software quirk instead of the facts of the incident. A data-entry UX failure was escalating into the criminal-justice system.
</PopUp>

None of this is internal tooling. BARD is the system of record under federal statute, and its output, the annual Recreational Boating Statistics in continuous publication since 1961, feeds national fatality rates, state grant allocations, and the rulemaking behind life-jacket and kill-switch regulations. If the system makes the data weaker, every decision built on that data gets weaker too.

## What the research actually found

I interviewed twenty jurisdiction points-of-contact across every volume tier, presented early work to the boating-law-administrator community at a NASBLA conference, and visited the Coast Guard's offices to watch the review-and-compliance side, meaning what happens to a case after a state hits submit. Most interviews were remote. The screen-shares were the unlock.

Watching people work, I kept seeing the same thing: a dozen tabs open. The original write-up in one, the responding officer's notes in another, the legacy form in a third, and somewhere a private sense of what the reviewer would want to see. Everyone was reconciling the same record across views the system refused to reconcile for them.

Then I looked at the data and found the loudest signal there is. The Coast Guard had built their own tools to survive the system. Because the legacy did no validation, reviewers were pulling and recombining fields by hand, out of more than 380 possible fields only 80 to 140 mattered for any given event, into shadow tools just to make a case readable. When your users build software to work around your software, the verdict is already in.

The veterans were blunt. A contractor who had spent twenty years inside BARD called it "an 1980s kinda look" and said the annual tables had looked "sort of the same since the 70s." The former Florida coordinator, the person who ran an entire state's reporting for over a decade, said state coordinators had "been wanting BARD to be improved for years." The formal record agreed. In a survey of fifty-three state users, only nineteen would affirm that BARD was intuitive to use. A system two-thirds of its users will not call usable is a system people have stopped expecting anything from.

<PopUp anchor="research">
The all-56-states form rule did not arrive as a flash. It accumulated, screen-share after screen-share, until I reached for a pattern from somewhere unexpected: Facebook's "View As," which lets you preview your profile the way the public sees it. The form needed the same move. One record, shown the way the state works and the way the Coast Guard reviews, without forcing either side to adapt to the other.
</PopUp>

## One thing loudly, everything else quietly

My guiding principle for the whole system: every surface should say exactly one thing loudly and everything else quietly, because the person reading it is on their fortieth case of the week, not their first. I evaluated against Nielsen's usability heuristics and held the system to Section 508 and WCAG 2.2 AA, computed rather than eyeballed, with contrast verified mathematically across every content-on-surface pairing in both light and dark modes. The work produced two patterns worth naming.

### The Insight Engine: making clean data speak

The legacy dashboard was tables. Nothing helped a user see a signal, which is exactly the actionable intelligence a Coast Guard analyst needs to move resources, and funding, early. So I built a system that reads the now-normalized data and renders a single plain-language insight at the top of each surface: *"Operator inattention remains Florida's leading factor this year."* *"Miami-Dade, Palm Beach, and Hillsborough account for 30% of Florida's incidents."*

<PopUp anchor="insight-engine">
The decision I am most sure about: I did not reach for AI. Once the forms were normalized, I could derive every insight deterministically. Calculate the shape, call it into the UI, done. In 2026 the reflex is to bolt an LLM onto everything. This needed the opposite. The power was not a model. It was data quality, and a model would only have added a way to be confidently wrong about a federal safety statistic.
</PopUp>

A deliberate counterweight: two modules, the quarterly Compliance Summary and the annual Recreational Boating Statistics, sit outside the engine entirely. No triggers, no rotation. They are anchors of fact, not editorial voice. Knowing which surfaces should speak and which should simply state is the part most teams get wrong.

### The form: one record, two readings

The second pattern answers what the screen-shares revealed. The form carries a single record but renders two views. *Your View*, ordered the way a state works, and *USCG View*, ordered the way a federal reviewer reads, so neither side has to adapt to the other. That same toggle is where the all-56-states rule lives: if any state populates a field in its real submissions, the field is preserved as optional rather than cut. I verified that rule against actual sample submissions from Illinois, Utah, and California.

Compliance is a live score, not a final-pass gate. As Maria fills sections, a Federal Compliance percentage climbs, so she can see which section will move the number most. The opposite of a system that lets a case look "complete" and then bounces it. Submission stays locked until the threshold is met.

<PopUp anchor="compliance">
The first instinct was a validator that checks everything at submit and lists the errors. I argued for the live score instead. A validator tells you you have already failed. A live score tells you how close you are and what to do next. For someone racing a sixty-day federal clock, that difference is the whole experience.
</PopUp>

Underneath both patterns is a design system built for a twenty-five-year horizon: a three-layer token architecture (raw primitives, mode-aware semantic tokens, component bindings) where status and sentiment are kept parallel rather than nested, so a status color can diverge from a sentiment color years from now without a refactor. An eight-state case lifecycle gives every workflow state its own hue, so color alone tells a reviewer who currently holds the case, fixing a legacy failure where "draft" and "submitted" looked nearly identical at table scale.

## What changed

Early design work went to the boating-law-administrator community at a NASBLA gathering in March 2026, and it landed with the state users who are BARD's core audience. That reception is the one outcome I can point to today. The rest ships at initial operating capability in 2027.

**Fifty-six jurisdictions, finally one system.** The territories were the clearest casualty of the old build: software made for the English-speaking mainland and extended outward without being designed for the places it had to serve. The rebuild starts from the opposite premise. One governing model, fifty-six first-class members of it. That premise is why the all-56-states form rule and the territory greeting pools exist, the architecture refusing to let any jurisdiction be an afterthought.

**One schema where there were fifty-six.** Collapsing the forms means a Florida officer's entry and a Utah officer's entry are finally comparable at the field level, because they are finally collected the same way. The national dataset stops papering over differences it was never built to reconcile.

Beyond the structure, the rebuild makes things possible the isolated legacy could not: cross-jurisdictional pattern detection at speed, a pre-submission compliance view that lets states catch federal gaps before a rejection, faster reconciliation of news-media signal against the filed record, and interoperability with the Coast Guard's broader case-management program, where BARD is one node in an effort replacing more than eleven legacy systems.

One thing I am careful not to claim: BARD does not save lives directly. It is the measurement and policy-input layer, not an intervention. The honest version is that stronger data makes stronger safety policy possible, and the causal chain runs through NASBLA and federal rulemaking, not from my interface to the water.

## What I got wrong, and what I still don't know

The thing that surprised me was being wrong in an instructive way. I assumed a lean form was a better form and ran an aggressive cut pass. On the People form alone, eighteen fields looked dead by every measure I had. Then I checked the cuts against real state submissions, Illinois, Utah, California, and the "dead" fields were populated. States were quietly relying on fields my wireframes implied did not exist. The surprise was not being wrong about a field. It was realizing I had let the wrong artifact answer the question: a wireframe shows render state, not field inventory. That reversal became a governing rule, one master form, and if any single state uses a field it stays, and it permanently demoted my wireframes to render-state authority only.

What I would do differently follows from that: write the preservation rule before the first cut, not after the first reversal. I learned the principle by violating it. And I would sequence the localization the other way, recruiting native speakers from the territories to author the greeting phrasing first, rather than drafting from convention and scheduling validation after. The most culturally sensitive part of the system is the part that shipped as draft-pending-confirmation, and that ordering was mine to fix.

<PopUp anchor="reflection">
The open question I cannot answer yet: I tuned the entire Insight Engine voice to one persona, the jurisdiction filer. The analyst and the executive surfaces do not exist yet. So I genuinely do not know whether the operational register holds for them, or whether each needs its own. Put another way, I do not yet know whether the voice I built belongs to the user or to the system. I will find out when those surfaces ship.
</PopUp>
