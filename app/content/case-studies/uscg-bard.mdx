<p className="section-lede">She runs boating-incident reporting for the highest-volume jurisdiction in the country. Fifteen to thirty cases a session, several sessions a week.</p>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">The bet</div>

## Standardize the data, not the people.

The three frictions share one root. Fifty-six ways of filing meant the system could hold the data and never read it.

The obvious fix, one national form, always died against fifty-six ways of working. So I bet the other way: leave every state's process alone, and unify only the record underneath. One filing, one picture, one number, without asking anyone to change.

</section>

## One form. Two audiences. Fifty-six states.

The form every jurisdiction now files into. It shows each state only the fields their case needs, scores federal compliance as they work instead of at the end, and lets a Coast Guard reviewer see exactly what the state sees, in the same screen.

<div className="eyebrow">Reflection</div>

## The hard part wasn't the design.

<p className="section-lede">Standardizing fifty-six state forms into one was an information architecture problem. Getting fifty-six jurisdictions to agree to it was the real work.</p>

I thought the win would be a leaner form. It was the opposite. Every state built its form for a reason, and none of them wanted to give it up. So I kept every state's fields and made them optional for everyone. No one lost anything; they gained a shared spine underneath.
