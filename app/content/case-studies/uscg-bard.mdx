<div className="eyebrow">The problem</div>

## Investigators have to get the case right. The system fought them.

**Boating accident cases are rising**, and every one becomes a federal record.

**The job isn't pushing forms.** Investigators have to get the details right, because the case is evidence.

**The legacy system worked against that.** The interface was clunky. Cases looked complete while missing required data. Federal mandates went unmet.

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">The bet</div>

## How might every state keep working its own way, and still add up to one national picture?

**Standardize the data, not the people.** All three frictions share one root. Fifty-six versions of the form meant no one could see across them. An investigator couldn't spot a trend. A reviewer couldn't act on one.

The obvious fix was one national form. It never survived contact with how people actually work. So I bet the other way: leave every state's process alone, and unify only the record underneath.

</section>

<section className="cs-section">

<div className="eyebrow">The solution</div>

## The form bends. The record doesn't.

Every state's fields live in one schema, and every field is optional. So an investigator opens the form and sees only what their case needs, nothing else. As they work, the form scores its own federal compliance, so nothing is missing at submission. And when the Coast Guard reviews it, they read the same record from their own view.

<div className="eyebrow">What it took</div>

## The hard part wasn't the design.

<p className="section-lede">Standardizing fifty-six state forms into one was an information architecture problem. Getting fifty-six jurisdictions to agree to it was the real work.</p>

I thought the win would be a leaner form. It was the opposite. Every state built its form for a reason, and none of them wanted to give it up. So I kept every state's fields and made them optional for everyone. No one lost anything; they gained a shared spine underneath.
